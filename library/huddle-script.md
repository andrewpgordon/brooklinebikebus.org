# What to say on the morning

Three short talks. The crew one at 7:20, the kids one at 7:26, and a few lines
to have ready at Beacon Street. Say them in your own words, but keep the order,
because the order is what people remember at 7:30 in the dark.

The kids' talk has to fit between 7:26 and 7:30, and it has to work for a
five-year-old. Four minutes is the ceiling. Read it out loud once the night
before and you'll find it runs shorter than it looks.

## 7:20, the crew. Ninety seconds.

Stand them in a circle away from the kids.

1. Read out every corner and who has it, and point in the direction of each one.
   Don't hand out a list. Say it.
2. Go round anyone with a job and ask out loud who has their kid for the ride.
   If somebody can't answer, take the job off them now and give it to a parent
   who is riding without a job.
3. Say the Beacon rule: nobody gets back on a bike until the sweep is across.
   Say it even though it's in the brief.
4. Point at the captain and the sweep so everyone knows which two names to
   shout.
5. One phone number for the morning, and it's the captain's. Phones in pockets,
   not in hands.

## 7:26, the kids. Four minutes, maximum.

Get them off the bikes and standing in front of you. Helmets already on.

> Good morning. I'm [name]. Can everybody hear me? Hand up if you can't.
>
> [If they're here] Those are Brookline police officers on the bikes at the back.
> They're riding with us this morning to help us get across Beacon Street.
>
> Here's where we're going. Round the park, up Griggs Road, a right turn onto
> Washington Street, and then we all cross Beacon Street together on one light.
> After that it's a few more minutes and we're at the bike racks at quarter to
> eight.
>
> Five things, then we go.
>
> **One.** I'm at the front. [Sweep's name] is at the very back. Stay between
> us. If you can see me and you can see [them], you're in the right place.
> Nobody rides in front of me, even if you're quick.
>
> **Two.** Some of these grown-ups are going to ride ahead of us and stand on
> corners, so that cars wait while we go past. That's [name], that's [name],
> that's [name]. Point at each one as you say it. They're not stopping for a
> rest, so don't stop with them. Ride past them and keep going.
>
> **Three.** We ride on the right-hand side of the road, going the same way as
> the cars. Two of you side by side at the most, never three.
>
> **Four.** When I stop, everybody stops. We stop at every light and every stop
> sign, and then we all go together. There's one place near the end of Griggs
> Road where we stop and wait for everybody before we turn onto Washington
> Street. If you get near the front and I'm stopped, you stop too.
>
> **Five.** You have two jobs. If a car comes up behind us, shout "car back".
> If you're slowing down, shout "stopping". Shout them, don't say them. Try it
> with me. Car back. Stopping.
>
> And if any of this gets hard, or your legs hurt, or you get scared, tell the
> nearest grown-up and we'll slow down. We'd much rather go slow.
>
> One more thing and it's the big one.
>
> When we get to Beacon Street we all stop at the curb, and then we go across
> together on one green light. There are train tracks in the middle of the road.
> Go straight over them. Don't turn your wheel on the tracks, don't stop on
> them, and keep pedalling until you're over. A wheel that goes sideways on a
> track will put you down.
>
> We go when I go, and nobody goes before me. If the light changes and you're
> still on this side, you stay on this side with [sweep] and you do not race
> across. We will all get over. And on the far side nobody rides on until I've
> counted everybody.
>
> Helmets buckled. Let's go.

### Why it's in that order

The police line comes first so that no kid spends the ride wondering about it.
Then where we're going, because a five-year-old who knows the shape of the
morning is a calmer rider.

The front and the back are rule one because it's the only rule that still works
if a kid forgets every other one. The corner people come next, because a kid who
stops next to a corker is a kid standing in the mouth of a side street. The two
shouts come late because kids will actually do them, and it makes the group
loud, which is most of why drivers notice us.

Beacon is last so it's the thing still in their heads when we roll out. Say the
pause before it. The pause is what makes them listen to it.

The ride page used to carry seven lines about how we ride and now carries four,
because most of them were things a parent enforces next to their own kid rather
than things a captain shouts (D21). What's left there is the pace requirement,
stay between the leader and the sweep, what a corker is doing, and say so if
it's hard. All four are in this talk.

## At the near curb, Washington Square

> Stop here. Stay back from the edge and stay on your bike.
>
> Straight over the tracks, keep pedalling, don't stop in the middle.
>
> We go when I go. If you don't get across, stay here with [sweep] and wait for
> the next one.

## On the far side, before anyone rides on

> Stop here. Nobody rides on until I've counted.
>
> [Count. Say the number.] Everybody's across. Off we go.

## At the racks

> That was [x] minutes. Lock up, grab your bag, and the next one is [date].

Tell them the date at the racks, even if it's only a guess, and put the same
date in the thank-you that goes out that afternoon. The gap between the first
ride and the second is where most bike buses quietly stop.
