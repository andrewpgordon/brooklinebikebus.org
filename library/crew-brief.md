# Crew brief

For the adults who have a job on ride day. Written for the Driscoll route, and
it should hold for any ride on it. Names for a particular ride are kept out of
this file on purpose (the sign-up sheet has them); this is the part that doesn't
change month to month.

## Read this first

Every child rides with their own parent or guardian. Having a job does not
change that. If your job takes you away from your own kid for part of the ride,
find the adult who will be beside them and say so out loud before we roll out.
If there isn't one, you don't have the job that morning, and saying that at 7:25
is completely normal.

Nobody here is supervising anyone else's child, and none of this makes us ride
marshals, instructors or officials. We are parents who agreed to stand in
particular places. The wording families signed says exactly that, and it stays
true on the day. See `docs/release-language.md`.

## The jobs

**Ride captain.** First bike. Takes the pace from the slowest kid behind them
rather than from the road. Calls the stops and does the counting. Nobody passes
the captain.

**Sweep.** Last bike, behind the last child. Carries the pump and the patch kit.
If the sweep stops, everything stops, so the sweep is the one person who can
halt the whole ride by standing still.

**Corkers.** Ride ahead to a side street and hold it while the group goes past,
so a driver doesn't pull out into the middle of the line. The next section is
all about this one.

**Crossing lead.** At Washington Square from about 7:30. Collects the families
joining there, then takes the group over Beacon Street on foot.

**Hill walker.** Meets the Corey Hill families at 7:00 at Summit and Jordan,
picks up more at 7:05 at Mason Terrace, and walks them down to Griggs Park for
7:15. Bikes are pushed the whole way, including down the footpath. Two adults is
better than one for this leg.

**Gather point.** At the park from 7:05. Gets the sign up and the pump out, hands
out vests, checks helmets, brakes and tyres as families arrive, and keeps a
headcount.

**Donuts** and **photos** both matter more than they sound like they do. The
photos are how the second ride gets more families than the first one did.

## What a corker actually does

You ride ahead of the group to a side street, stop at the mouth of it, and make
yourself big and obvious. Stay astride the bike with a foot down so you can move.
Catch the eye of any driver waiting there and point at the group coming. Most
people wait. Thank them.

What you are not doing is stopping traffic. You have no authority to stop
anybody. You are a parent in a bright vest asking a driver for fifteen seconds.
If a car comes anyway, get yourself out of the way and let it through, and the
group behind you waits. Never put a bike or a body in front of a moving car, and
never touch a car.

Stay until the sweep passes you. The sweep going by is your signal, not the last
kid you happen to see.

On this route the corners are close enough to the start that you can usually get
to a second one without passing anybody, by going at the beginning rather than
after the group. If your two do come up too close together to make, say so at
the 7:20 huddle and we'll hand the second one to somebody else. Riding up the
outside of a line of children is the thing to avoid.

Distances are measured along the route line on the website map, from the park.
Nobody has stood at these corners at 7:35 on a school day yet. That's the
Saturday pre-ride, so until then this is a draft and the order is the useful part.

|---|---|---|
| 0 m | Griggs Park, NE corner | Start. The footpath from Marion Street comes down here. |

**Nothing is held after Beacon Street.** Andrew's call, Oct 1: by the time the
group is over Beacon it's three hundred metres from the racks and the last
stretch of Washington Street doesn't need holding. Salisbury Road and Evans Road
were in an earlier version of this table and came out. The useful side effect is
that no corker ever has to get past the group, so nobody is riding up the
outside of twenty children.

So once the group is across Beacon, the corkers are ordinary riders. Fall in and
ride to school.

Two things that aren't in the table and want eyes on the Saturday ride.
Driveways and the parking exits around Washington Square don't show up as
streets on a map, and there are several. And we don't know which side of
Washington Street the racks are on. If they're on the far side, the group has one
more crossing at 7:45 in school-run traffic, which would put one position back
on that last stretch after all.

## Beacon Street at Marion, on the walking leg

This one happens at about 7:10, twenty minutes before anybody hears the talk at
the park, which is why the hill walker gets thirty seconds of their own at 7:00.

The lights are button-operated on each half of Beacon with the trolley tracks
in between, and the tracks themselves are an uncontrolled crossing. Whether
that works as one light or two has not been checked on the ground.

- The front adult crosses first and waits on the far side.
- The second adult holds the curb with everyone still waiting.
- Nobody stops or waits on the tracks. If the group can't clear them in one go,
  the rest wait on the sidewalk for the next light.
- The zebra crossing further down Marion Street has no lights at all. Wait for a
  gap and cross as one group.
- Bikes are pushed the whole way, including down the footpath into the park,
  which is about 10% at the top and can be slippery.

## Washington Square

The captain stops the group on the near curb, well back from the corner, and
everybody gets off. Bikes are pushed. One light if we fit, and with thirty-odd
bikes and riders we probably don't.

**There are town crossing guards here, one on each side of Washington Street.**
They are the only people on this route who can actually stop traffic. A corker
asks; a crossing guard holds up a paddle and cars stop. So at this crossing we
work to them, not the other way round. Say good morning, tell them how many
people are coming over and that everyone is on foot, and then do what they say.
Nobody from our crew directs traffic while a guard is standing there.

Tell them before the day. Thirty bikes arriving at somebody's post at 7:38
unannounced is a rotten surprise, and a guard who knows we're coming is the best
help we have. Also find out what time they come on post, because 7:38 is
twenty-two minutes before the bell and we have never checked.

Build the crossing so it works if a guard is off sick, which is the same reason
nothing here depends on the police. Without them, two of our own people stand at
the crossing instead, one watching each carriageway.

What happens when we don't fit:

- The crossing lead goes first with the front of the group and stops on the far
  side. The front does not ride away.
- Everyone still waiting stays on the curb with the sweep, back from the edge.
- Nobody steps off the curb on a flashing hand. Nobody runs.
- Nobody gets back on a bike until the sweep is across and says so. That now
  includes the corkers, since there's nothing to hold on the far side.
- A guard waving us over is not a reason to send the group in two halves. If
  the whole group can't go, the rest wait for the next light.

That last line is the most important sentence in this brief. The thing that
goes wrong at this crossing is half the group on one side of Beacon Street and
half on the other, with the front of the ride already rolling away.

We dismount and walk at Beacon whether or not there's a police officer or a
crossing guard standing there. That rule doesn't move for a good morning, a
quiet light, or somebody in a vest waving us across.

## Where we wait for each other

The captain stops and counts at four places, and says the number out loud:

1. Griggs Road at Washington Street, before the turn.
2. The near curb at Washington Square.
3. The far side of Beacon, after everyone is across.
4. The racks.

Between those, the only rule is to stay between the captain and the sweep.

## Calls and signals

Adults do the hand signals. Kids keep both hands on the bars. A six-year-old
taking a hand off to point is worse than no signal at all.

Kids have two things to shout, and they should actually shout them. "Car back"
when something is coming up behind. "Stopping" when they're slowing down.

Adults use those two as well, plus "clear" from a corker holding a corner, and
"hold" from anyone, which means stop now and is the only word that outranks the
captain.

## When something goes wrong

A kid falls. The nearest adult stops with them, their own adult comes to them,
the sweep stops, and the captain holds the front. Don't crowd a crying child.

A flat tyre or a dropped chain. The child's own adult takes them and the bike up
onto the sidewalk and either fixes it there or walks them in. The group carries
on, because we don't hold a street full of kids for a repair. The sweep hands
over the pump and the patch kit on the way past and then stays at the back of
the group, which is the one place the sweep has to be.

A kid can't keep up. We slow down. That is the arrangement, and the kid isn't
the problem. If it still isn't working, that family steps onto the sidewalk and
walks the rest with their own adult. Plenty of kids will walk a bit of it.

A driver won't wait. Let them go. Nobody argues with a car while a group of
children is watching.

The group splits. Whoever is at the back of the front half stops and waits.
The front does not push on to protect the schedule. Getting there at 7:50
together is a fine outcome.

Somebody is late to the park. The captain leaves at 7:30. Latecomers ride with
their own adult or join at Washington Square at 7:38.

## Police

Treat any officer positions as a proposal until they say otherwise. Ask them
where they want to be, and tell them the two places we think are worst: the
merge onto Washington Street and the Beacon crossing at Washington Square.

Build the morning so it still works if they get called away, which can happen at
7:35 on any day. Nothing in this brief depends on them being there.
